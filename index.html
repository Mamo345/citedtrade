How the US duty on a pair of shoes is built

Three things decide what a pair of imported shoes pays at the US border: what the upper is made of, what the pair costs, and where it was made. The first two set the base rate, which rarely changes. The third adds duties that changed three times in 2026.

A free guide. Select any underlined figure to see its source.

The base rate: material and price per pair

Every shoe has a base rate in the US tariff schedule (HTS). It depends on what the upper is made of, how the shoe is built, who it is for and what one pair is worth. These rates rarely change. The duties added on top are what keep moving.

The cheaper the pair, the higher the rate. At the $12 line, the extra 90 cents a pair makes a cheaper shoe pay more duty in dollars than a slightly more expensive one. At today’s rates, a pair from Vietnam valued at $12.00 pays $4.80, and one valued at $12.01 pays $3.90.Note 5

Within each band some shoes have lines of their own. At $6.50 to $12, sneakers whose outer sole touches the ground mostly with textile pay 7.5% or 12.5% instead of 90 cents plus 20%, under conditions your broker can check.Source 6 Lower down, shoes whose soles are only glued to the upper have separate lines. Rubber and plastic footwear that covers the ankle (6402.91) follows a similar ladder.

Two lessons. At the $12 line, a pair worth one cent more pays 90 cents less duty. At $30, a men’s sneaker with a leather upper pays $3.45 less per pair from Vietnam than one with a textile upper. On 10,000 pairs a year, that is $9,000 and $34,500.

Vietnam adds 12.5% to the base rate. China adds 12.5% plus the older 7.5%, where that duty covers the code.

$12.00, line 6404.11.89, Vietnam: $0.90 + $12.00 × 32.5% = $4.80.

$12.01, line 6404.11.90, Vietnam: $12.01 × 32.5% = $3.90. China: $12.01 × 40% = $4.80.

$30.00, line 6404.11.90, Vietnam: $30 × 32.5% = $9.75. China: $30 × 40% = $12.00.

$30.00, line 6403.99.60, Vietnam: $30 × (8.5% + 12.5%) = $6.30.

The $12 step: $4.80 − $3.90 = $0.90 a pair, $9,000 on 10,000 pairs.

Leather against textile: $9.75 − $6.30 = $3.45 a pair, $34,500 on 10,000 pairs.

What does not help: foreign-trade zones

Foreign-trade zones let importers delay or reduce duties in some cases. For the new Section 301 duty, US Customs says goods can enter a zone only in “privileged foreign status”, so a zone does not get them out of it.Source 22

What to watch

  • Lawsuits against the new duties. Small businesses, including Burlap and Barrel and Collective Horology, and 25 states (since August 3, 2026) are challenging the Section 301 forced-labor duties in the Court of International Trade.Source 23 If the duties are struck down, refunds may follow, as they did for the IEEPA tariffs.
  • Section 122 refunds. The surcharge was ruled unlawful, but that ruling is stayed pending appeal. Mattel says the availability, timing and amount of refunds remain uncertain.Source 24
  • Your records. Keep entry records for every shipment, with dates, HTS codes and duties paid. If refunds open up, that is what a claim will need.

Nine questions for your customs broker

They follow from the rules above and from what public companies disclosed. They are prompts for your own team, not legal advice.

  1. Which HTS code do you use for each of our shoes, and why that line rather than the next one?

  2. Are any of our shoes close to a price step, such as $12 a pair for textile sneakers?

  3. For our shoes from China, does the 7.5% Section 301 duty apply to each code?

  4. Have you claimed refunds on every entry that paid IEEPA tariffs?

    Wolverine filed claims for $34.0 million after paying $35.9 million. Its filing does not say why.Source 25

  5. Where do our refund claims stand, and when should the money arrive?

    Steven Madden filed on April 20, 2026, the day the refund system opened. By the end of June it had received $89.0 million of the $90.1 million it paid.Source 26

  6. Is everything set up for Customs to pay refunds to our account?

  7. If a factory shared our tariff costs, do we owe it part of any refund?

    Deckers reports its refunds net of the amounts it shares with its manufacturers.Source 27

  8. Did we raise prices because of tariffs?

    Deckers and Mattel both report lawsuits from consumers over their tariff refunds.Source 27Source 28

  9. Are we keeping the records we would need if the Section 122 or Section 301 duties are struck down?

Paste them into an email to your broker. A tariff check answers the ones about rates with your own numbers.
